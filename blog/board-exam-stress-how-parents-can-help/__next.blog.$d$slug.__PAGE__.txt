17:Td7d,<p>Board exams are a big moment, and some nervousness is normal — a little pressure can even help a child focus. But when worry starts affecting sleep, appetite or confidence, the way parents respond can make a real difference.</p>
<h2 id="notice-the-signs-early">Notice the signs early</h2>
<p>Stress doesn’t always look like stress. Watch for:</p>
<ul>
<li>Trouble sleeping, or sleeping far more than usual.</li>
<li>Changes in appetite.</li>
<li>Irritability, tearfulness or withdrawing from family.</li>
<li>Avoiding study entirely, or studying late into the night without progress.</li>
<li>Frequent headaches or stomach aches before study or tests.</li>
</ul>
<p>One of these on its own is common. Several together, lasting more than a week or two, is worth a gentle conversation.</p>
<h2 id="talk-about-effort-not-just-marks">Talk about effort, not just marks</h2>
<p>Children often feel that their results will decide how their parents see them. Make it clear that you value their effort, not only the final score. Try questions like “What felt easier today?” instead of “How many marks will you get?”</p>
<p>Avoid comparing them with siblings, cousins or classmates. Comparison rarely motivates; it usually adds pressure.</p>
<h2 id="build-a-steady-routine">Build a steady routine</h2>
<p>A predictable day reduces anxiety. Help your child plan:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Fixed study blocks</strong> of 45–60 minutes, with short breaks in between.</li>
<li><strong>Regular meals</strong> — skipping meals makes concentration harder.</li>
<li><strong>Sleep</strong> — late-night cramming usually costs more than it gains. A rested brain remembers better.</li>
<li><strong>Movement</strong> — even a short walk helps clear the mind.</li>
</ul>
<h2 id="break-the-syllabus-into-small-wins">Break the syllabus into small wins</h2>
<p>A whole syllabus feels impossible. A single chapter doesn’t. Help your child split revision into small, clear goals for each day, and tick them off. Visible progress is one of the best cures for panic.</p>
<h2 id="practise-under-exam-conditions">Practise under exam conditions</h2>
<p>Much exam stress is fear of the unknown. Timed practice with past papers or sample papers makes the real exam feel familiar. Afterwards, focus on what to improve rather than the score.</p>
<h2 id="keep-screens-and-news-in-check">Keep screens and news in check</h2>
<p>Constant chat about exams, rumours about papers and late-night scrolling all raise anxiety. Agree on sensible screen breaks, especially in the hour before sleep.</p>
<h2 id="be-the-calm-in-the-room">Be the calm in the room</h2>
<p>Children read their parents’ mood closely. If you are anxious, they will feel it. Keep your tone calm, keep meals and family time normal, and remind them that one exam does not define their future.</p>
<h2 id="when-to-get-extra-support">When to get extra support</h2>
<p>If your child is stuck on particular topics, a patient one-on-one tutor can rebuild confidence quickly by clearing specific doubts. And if stress seems severe or persistent — ongoing sleeplessness, panic, or low mood — speak to the school counsellor or your family doctor. Asking for help early is a strength, not a weakness.</p>
<h2 id="the-one-thing-to-remember">The one thing to remember</h2>
<p>Your child needs to know you are on their side, whatever the result. That reassurance is often the most powerful exam support of all.</p>
